FIRST, A DIFFERENT WAY OF SEEING

Forget the photograph.
Think of an echo.

Ordinary satellite cameras collect sunlight reflected from the ground. Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) makes its own signal: it sends microwave pulses towards Earth, measures what comes back, and uses those echoes to construct an image.

WHY “SYNTHETIC”?

The satellite builds a bigger antenna
by moving.

As it travels along its orbit, SAR observes the same patch of ground from successive positions. Precise processing combines the phase history of those returns, synthesising an antenna much larger than the physical one.

visual language.

These are typical tendencies, not fixed rules. Radar brightness depends on wavelength, viewing angle, geometry, moisture and surface roughness.

Calm water

Often dark: the smooth surface reflects energy away from the satellite.

Built-up areas

Often bright: walls and streets can create strong corner reflections.

Forests

Complex returns from leaves, branches and trunks reveal vegetation structure.

Ice and glaciers

Radar helps track ice structure, movement and changing boundaries.

THE NEXT LEVEL

InSAR can measure a landscape moving.

Interferometric SAR compares the phase of radar observations from different passes. Under suitable conditions, it can reveal tiny changes in the distance between satellite and ground — sometimes down to millimetres or centimetres.

05 — THE MARS QUESTION

Did Viking use SAR
in the 1970s?

No. And it's worth getting that right.

Viking 1 and Viking 2 reached Mars in 1976 with orbiters, landers, cameras and other scientific instruments. They did not carry Synthetic Aperture Radar.

The later Mars missions carried radar sounders. MARSIS on Mars Express and SHARAD on Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter use related radar principles to probe beneath the surface. They are not conventional side-looking Earth-observation SAR systems.

Different instruments. Related physics. A much deeper question.

THE BIG PICTURE

Sometimes the most interesting things
are the things we can't see.

From a radar experiment in the 1950s to the oceans of Earth and the buried geology of Mars, SAR changed what an image could mean. Not every picture begins with light.
